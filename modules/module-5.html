1. Case method of learning

A business case presents a situation, decision, problem or opportunity faced by an organisation or manager. The case method asks learners to interpret the facts, identify the central issue, evaluate alternatives and recommend a defensible course of action. The purpose is not to guess a hidden “correct” answer; it is to reason from evidence and explain the trade-offs.

Why use the case method?

  • Connects concepts with realistic managerial situations.
  • Develops problem identification, analysis and decision-making.
  • Encourages discussion, evidence-based reasoning and perspective-taking.
  • Builds confidence in explaining and defending recommendations.

2. Structured case analysis

  1. Read and understand: identify organisation, context, people, timeline and constraints.
  2. Define the central issue: state the decision or problem in one or two sentences.
  3. Separate facts from assumptions: note what is known, missing or uncertain.
  4. Analyse causes: use relevant course concepts, evidence and stakeholder perspectives.
  5. Generate alternatives: identify realistic options, not just the first idea.
  6. Evaluate alternatives: compare benefits, costs, risks, feasibility, ethics and time.
  7. Recommend: choose an option and justify it using evidence.
  8. Implement: state actions, owners, timeline, communication and success measures.

Answer template: Situation → Central issue → Analysis → Alternatives → Recommendation → Implementation → Risks / measures.

3. Case preparation and presentation

Preparation best practices: read the case more than once; annotate facts; build a timeline; identify stakeholders; check calculations; use relevant frameworks; divide team responsibilities; prepare evidence and anticipate questions.

Presentation structure: brief context, central issue, key analysis, alternatives, recommendation, implementation plan and risks. Use readable slides, clear charts, limited text, consistent terminology and source citations. Rehearse transitions and timing. Every team member should understand the recommendation.

Common challenges: information overload, incomplete data, confirmation bias, dominant team members, weak time management, unsupported assumptions, unclear slide design and inability to answer questions. Address these with role allocation, evidence logs, rehearsal and a question-handling plan.

4. Etiquette in managerial communication

Etiquette is the set of professional behaviours and conventions that show respect, consideration and credibility in workplace communication. It applies to face-to-face, written, telephone, meeting and digital interactions.

  • Respect: use courteous language; do not ridicule, interrupt or demean.
  • Listening: give attention, allow others to finish and clarify before responding.
  • Timeliness: arrive prepared, meet deadlines and respond within reasonable time.
  • Written etiquette: use clear subject lines, appropriate salutations, concise wording and proofread messages.
  • Meeting etiquette: review the agenda, contribute relevantly, manage devices and respect time.
  • Digital etiquette: use professional tone, protect confidential information and avoid unnecessary reply-all messages.
  • Cross-cultural respect: ask rather than assume; adapt to context without stereotyping.

Advantages: trust, smoother coordination, positive professional image, reduced conflict and stronger working relationships.

5. Legal aspects of communication

This section introduces the syllabus topics for learning and awareness; it is not legal advice. Applicable law depends on jurisdiction and facts.

Invasion of privacy

Occurs when communication or information handling intrudes improperly into a person's private life or discloses personal information without an appropriate basis. Examples include sharing private employee or customer information in an open group, circulating personal images without permission, or exposing confidential records. Good practice: collect only necessary information, restrict access, obtain appropriate consent, use secure channels and follow organisational policy and applicable law.

Misrepresentation

A false or misleading statement or presentation of facts that may influence another person’s decision. Examples include exaggerating product performance, hiding material limitations, altering figures or presenting unverified claims as established facts. Good practice: verify claims, distinguish fact from opinion, disclose material limitations and correct errors promptly.

Fraud in communication

Communication may be used to deceive someone for an improper gain or to cause loss. Examples can include impersonation, fraudulent payment instructions, forged approvals or deceptive requests for credentials. Good practice: verify identity and payment changes through a separate trusted channel, use approval controls, preserve records and report suspected fraud through authorised channels.

Ethical communication checklist

  • Is the information accurate and verifiable?
  • Have I disclosed relevant facts and limitations?
  • Am I authorised to share this information?
  • Does the message protect privacy and confidentiality?
  • Could the recipient reasonably be misled?
  • Is the communication respectful and properly documented?
