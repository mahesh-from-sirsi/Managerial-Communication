Meaning, definition and nature of communication Communication is the process of sharing information, ideas, opinions, feelings or instructions between people through words, symbols, behaviour or other channels, with the aim of creating common understanding and achieving a purpose. In management, communication is not complete merely because a sender has delivered a message. The receiver must interpret it sufficiently accurately for the intended understanding or action to occur. Key features / nature Two-way and interactive Effective communication involves a response or feedback, not just one-way delivery. Purposeful Messages inform, persuade, instruct, coordinate, motivate or solve problems. Continuous Managers communicate before, during and after decisions and activities. Dynamic and contextual Meaning can change with the situation, relationship, culture and channel. Verbal and non-verbal Words, tone, facial expressions, posture and gestures influence meaning. Symbolic and shared People use language, signs, numbers, images and gestures; shared meanings matter. Workplace example: “Submit the report soon.” A manager emails this instruction. One employee submits the report that evening; another assumes Friday. The message has been sent, but the deadline is ambiguous. A better instruction is: “Upload the sales report to the shared folder by 4.00 p.m. on Thursday, 12 November.” The second message reduces uncertainty. Remember the distinction: Information transmission means sending data or a message. Communication is broader: it includes interpretation, shared meaning and feedback. 2. Role and purpose of communication Communication is a core managerial activity. It connects organisational objectives with the work performed by individuals and teams. Purpose / role What it does Managerial example Inform Shares facts, policies, instructions, updates or knowledge. A manager informs staff about a revised attendance policy. Persuade Influences attitudes, opinions or actions through reasons and evidence. A team leader explains why a new quality process should be adopted. Instruct / direct Explains what must be done, by whom, how and by when. A supervisor allocates daily production targets. Coordinate Aligns the work of people, teams and departments. Marketing and operations coordinate stock before a festival sale. Motivate Encourages effort, commitment and confidence. A manager recognises good performance and explains team goals. Build relationships Develops trust, cooperation and a professional climate. A manager holds regular one-to-one discussions. Control Communicates standards, reviews results and initiates corrective action. A sales head discusses actual sales against targets. Support decisions Collects information, compares alternatives and communicates decisions. A manager consults finance and operations before approving a project. The supplied reference particularly highlights communication to inform and to persuade. In a managerial answer, explain these two purposes and show how they also support coordination, direction, motivation and control. 3. Classification of communication Communication can be classified on different bases. State the basis first; do not mix categories from different bases into one unstructured list. 3.1 Based on participants Type Meaning Example Intrapersonal Communication within oneself; internal thinking, reflection or self-talk. A manager privately evaluates two project options. Interpersonal Communication between two individuals. A manager gives performance feedback to an employee. Group Communication among members of a small group. A project team discusses task allocation. Mass Communication directed to a large and dispersed audience. A company-wide public announcement or press statement. 3.2 Based on mode / medium Type Meaning Examples Oral / spoken Message communicated through spoken words. Meeting, interview, phone call, presentation. Written Message communicated through written words or text. Email, memo, report, circular, notice. Non-verbal Meaning communicated through cues other than words, often accompanying verbal messages. Facial expression, posture, eye contact, gesture, tone of voice. Verbal communication is often divided into oral and written forms. Non-verbal cues can reinforce, contradict or modify spoken words. For example, saying “I am happy with the proposal” while displaying an irritated expression may create doubt. 3.3 Based on organisational formality Formal communication Follows officially recognised organisational channels. Examples: policy circular, official report and scheduled review meeting. Informal communication Arises through personal and social relationships. Workplace chat and the grapevine may spread information quickly, but can also distort it. 3.4 Other useful classifications By direction: downward, upward, horizontal (lateral) and diagonal communication. By feedback: one-way communication (no immediate response expected) and two-way communication (response and clarification are possible). Communication Classification bases → Participants Intra / inter / group / mass → Mode Oral / written / non-verbal Formality Formal / informal • Direction Down / up / lateral / diagonal • Feedback One-way / two-way Figure 1. Classification of communication by different bases. These are separate ways of classifying communication. Exam tip: A strong classification answer uses a small tree diagram, states each basis, and explains each category with one example. 4. Communication process The communication process explains how an idea is converted into a message, sent through a channel, interpreted by a receiver and checked through feedback. Sender Initiates → Encoding Converts idea → Message Content → Channel Medium Receiver Target person → Decoding Interprets → Understanding Meaning formed ↶ Feedback returns to the sender and helps check understanding. NOISE may interfere at any stage of the process. Figure 2. The communication process, showing feedback and noise. Element Explanation Example: monthly sales target Sender Person or source that initiates communication. Sales manager. Idea Thought, information or intention the sender wants to convey. Increase monthly sales by 10%. Encoding Converting the idea into words, symbols, figures or gestures. Preparing a clear target email. Message Actual content being communicated. Target, deadline and performance expectations. Channel Medium used to carry the message. Email, meeting, phone or video call. Receiver Person or group for whom the message is intended. Sales team. Decoding Interpreting and assigning meaning to the message. Employees understand the target and deadline. Feedback Receiver's response that helps the sender assess understanding. Team acknowledges the target and asks questions. Noise Any factor that interferes with transmission or interpretation. Poor audio, jargon, distraction or assumptions. 4.1 Types of noise Type Meaning and example Physical noise External disturbance, such as construction noise during a meeting. Technical / channel noise Poor internet connection, microphone failure or an unreadable attachment. Semantic noise Different interpretations of words, abbreviations or technical terms. Psychological noise Stress, anger, fear, prejudice or lack of trust affecting interpretation. Physiological noise Hearing difficulty, illness, fatigue or other physical conditions affecting communication. 4.2 Linear and two-way communication Linear / one-way A sender transmits a message to a receiver. It may suit a simple announcement, but does not by itself confirm understanding. Two-way Feedback allows the receiver to ask questions, clarify doubts and respond. It is useful for instructions, change communication, performance discussions and problem-solving. Worked example: internal assessment marks A college principal asks faculty members to submit internal assessment marks by a specified date. The principal is the sender; the instruction is encoded and sent through official email; faculty members receive and decode it. They acknowledge the email and ask whether marks should be entered in the portal or sent as a spreadsheet. This feedback prevents errors. An unclear deadline or broken portal link would be noise. For a 10-mark answer: Draw the process diagram, explain all elements in sequence, distinguish feedback from noise, and provide one complete example. 5. Characteristics of successful communication: The Seven Cs The supplied Chaturvedi reference presents the Seven Cs as practical conditions for effective communication. Use the exact terminology below when answering from this reference. Seven C Meaning Example / application Candidness Honest, sincere and fair communication; avoid bias or deliberate concealment. Explain the actual reason for a project delay rather than unfairly blaming an individual. Clarity Use familiar, precise words and organise ideas logically. Replace “finish it soon” with “submit by 3 p.m. on Monday.” Completeness Provide all information the receiver needs to understand or act. An event instruction includes date, venue, reporting time, dress code and contact person. Conciseness Use only the words necessary; avoid repetition without omitting essential details. A short email with a clear subject, action and deadline. Concreteness Be specific and definite rather than vague or abstract. “Complaints fell from 40 to 25” instead of “complaints reduced significantly.” Correctness Ensure facts, figures, names, grammar and details are accurate. Verify the meeting date, attachment and recipient list before sending. Courtesy Use a respectful, considerate and professional tone. “Please share the revised file” rather than “You failed to send it again.” 1. Candidness Be honest and fair. 2. Clarity Make meaning easy to understand. 3. Completeness Include what the receiver needs. 4. Conciseness Remove unnecessary words. 5. Concreteness Be specific and definite. 6. Correctness Check facts and language. 7. Courtesy Be respectful and professional. Figure 3. The Seven Cs of successful communication. The Seven Cs reinforce one another. A message may be concise but incomplete; it may be clear but factually incorrect. Effective communication requires an appropriate balance. Exam tip: Explain all seven Cs with a one-line meaning and a practical example. Do not substitute a different list of “Cs”; retain the terminology used in these notes. 6. Importance of communication in management Communication connects managerial functions and enables people to understand organisational goals, responsibilities, standards and results. Management function How communication supports it Example Planning Communicates objectives, assumptions, priorities, plans and deadlines. Management explains the annual sales plan. Organising Clarifies roles, responsibilities, authority and reporting relationships. A project manager assigns responsibilities to team members. Staffing Supports recruitment, selection, induction, training and performance feedback. HR explains job expectations to a new employee. Directing / leading Provides instructions, coaching, motivation and leadership. A manager explains a new process and encourages adoption. Controlling Communicates standards, compares actual performance with targets and explains corrective action. A production manager reviews defect rates and corrective steps. Communication also supports decision-making, coordination between departments, change management, employee engagement, customer relationships and organisational learning. Example: introduction of a new billing system Management must explain why the system is changing, train employees, gather feedback, coordinate IT and finance, and communicate how problems will be resolved. Without communication, even a technically sound change may fail. Exam tip: Organise the answer around the five management functions—planning, organising, staffing, directing and controlling—then add decision-making and coordination. 7. Communication structure in an organisation Organisational communication structure describes how information flows between people, levels and departments. 7.1 Communication by direction Top management ↓ Middle management ↓ Employees ↑ Upward communication: reports, suggestions, concerns and feedback Marketing ↔ Operations ↔ Finance Horizontal communication across similar levels • Diagonal communication across functions and levels Figure 4. Common directions of organisational communication (illustrative). Flow Direction Purpose and example Downward Higher level to lower level. Policies, instructions, targets and performance expectations; e.g., a department head communicates a revised timetable. Upward Lower level to higher level. Reports, suggestions, concerns and feedback; e.g., employees report a recurring customer complaint. Horizontal / lateral Between people or departments at the same or similar level. Coordination; e.g., marketing and operations agree on product availability. Diagonal Across departments and different hierarchical levels. Direct coordination; e.g., a finance analyst contacts a project manager in another department to clarify budget data. 7.2 Formal and informal structures Formal Follows approved channels and provides accountability, consistency and an official record. Informal Develops through social relationships and workplace interaction. It can be quick and useful, but rumours and distortion may spread when formal communication is delayed or incomplete. 7.3 Common organisational network patterns Supplementary concept Network Description Possible strength / limitation Chain Information moves through a sequence of reporting relationships. Clear hierarchy; may be slow and may filter information. Wheel A central person communicates with all other members. Fast central coordination; dependence on one person. Circle Each member communicates mainly with adjacent members. Can support participation; information may take longer to circulate. All-channel Members communicate freely with one another. Useful for collaborative problem-solving; can become difficult to coordinate in large groups. These network patterns are useful supplementary concepts for understanding how communication is organised. Prioritise downward, upward, horizontal and formal/informal communication in the main examination answer. Identify the direction: A sales employee reports a customer issue to the sales manager—upward. The sales manager gives a new target to the employee—downward. Sales and logistics managers coordinate a delivery—horizontal. A junior finance analyst contacts a senior project manager in another function—diagonal. 8. Communication in conflict resolution Conflict occurs when individuals or groups perceive differences in goals, interests, values, expectations or access to resources. Communication can either intensify conflict or help parties understand the issue and work towards a solution. The supplied Chaturvedi reference explains the S-TLC approach: Stop, Think, Listen and Communicate. Conflict resolution requires collaborative effort; one party's communication competence alone may not be sufficient. STOP Pause before reacting → THINK Consider the issue → LISTEN Understand the other view → COMMUNICATE Discuss and agree action Figure 5. S-TLC approach to conflict resolution. Step What to do Workplace illustration Stop Pause before reacting. Regain self-control and avoid an impulsive response. A manager does not reply angrily to a critical email. Think Consider the issue, background, goals, options, timing and the other person's perspective. Separate the issue from the person. The manager checks whether a missed deadline resulted from unclear ownership or a genuine delay. Listen Hear the other party patiently. Do not assume you already know their reasons. The manager asks each team member to explain the dependency that caused the delay. Communicate Express concerns clearly and respectfully; discuss options and agree on action. The team agrees on revised ownership, deadlines and a weekly progress check. Helpful communication behaviours during conflict Focus on the problem or behaviour, not on attacking the person. Use “I” statements: “I was concerned because I did not receive the figures before the review.” Ask open questions: “What prevented the report from being completed?” Listen actively and paraphrase: “So your team was waiting for the inventory data—is that correct?” Agree on specific next steps, responsibilities and timelines. Example: delayed customer order Two employees disagree about responsibility for a delayed order. Instead of assigning blame immediately, the manager pauses, reviews the order timeline, listens to both employees, identifies the hand-off failure and agrees on a clearer escalation process. Exam tip: Remember the exact sequence S-TLC. Explain why each step matters and show how it changes a blame-oriented conversation into collaborative problem-solving. 9. Communication in crisis management A crisis is an unexpected or serious situation that threatens people, operations, reputation, assets or the continuity of an organisation. Crisis communication is the timely, accurate and coordinated sharing of information with affected stakeholders. 9.1 Steps in crisis communication Verify facts: establish what has happened, what is affected and what is still unknown. Identify stakeholders: employees, customers, suppliers, regulators, media and the public, as relevant. Coordinate responsibility: identify the authorised spokesperson or communication owner. Communicate promptly: do not allow avoidable silence to create confusion or rumours. State known and unknown information: do not speculate or make unsupported promises. Give clear instructions: explain what stakeholders should do, avoid or expect. Provide regular updates: state when and where the next update will be shared. Review and recover: correct inaccurate information, explain recovery steps and learn from the incident. Verify facts → Identify stakeholders → Coordinate owner → Communicate Give instructions → Update regularly → Review & recover Figure 6. A practical sequence for crisis communication. 9.2 Principles of effective crisis communication Principle Why it matters Speed with accuracy A prompt response matters, but unverified information can worsen the crisis. Clarity People need understandable information and practical instructions. Consistency Different departments should not issue contradictory messages. Empathy Acknowledge the impact on affected people; avoid sounding defensive. Transparency Explain what is known, what is not known and what is being done. Regular updates Tell stakeholders when they can expect further information. Example: online examination system failure If a university's online examination portal becomes unavailable during an examination, it should verify the technical issue, identify affected students and centres, issue an authorised notice, explain whether students should wait or stop the attempt, clarify how lost time or submissions will be handled, and announce the next update time. It should not speculate about the cause before verification. Other suitable examples include a product recall, workplace accident, cyberattack, data leak, natural disaster or sudden supply-chain disruption. Exam tip: Distinguish facts, unknowns, immediate action and the next update. A crisis message should not make promises the organisation cannot guarantee. 10. Communication and negotiation Negotiation is a communication process in which two or more parties discuss their needs, interests, constraints and possible options to reach an agreement or manage a disagreement. 10.1 Stages of negotiation Preparation Objectives, facts, limits → Discussion Questions, listening, proposals → Agreement Terms and responsibilities → Implementation Follow-up and review Figure 7. Stages of negotiation. Stage Communication activities Example Preparation Identify the issue, objectives, interests, facts, alternatives, limits and people involved. A supplier and buyer review delivery data, quality requirements and acceptable prices before meeting. Discussion / negotiation Ask questions, listen, clarify interests, make proposals, explain reasons and explore trade-offs. The buyer requests a lower price; the supplier explains higher raw-material costs; both explore volume discounts. Agreement and implementation Record agreed terms, responsibilities, deadlines and follow-up arrangements. Both parties confirm price, quantity, delivery dates, payment terms and escalation process. 10.2 Distributive and integrative negotiation Supplementary concept Approach Meaning Example Distributive Often treated as a fixed-pie situation: each party tries to secure a larger share of limited value. Buyer and seller bargain over the price of a single item. Integrative Parties explore interests and options to create mutual value rather than focusing only on one demand. A buyer offers a longer contract and predictable volume in exchange for better pricing and service levels. A position is what a party says it wants; an interest is the underlying reason. A supplier's position may be “We cannot reduce the price.” Its interest may be protecting its margin because input costs have increased. Understanding interests can reveal options such as volume commitments, phased delivery or revised payment terms. 10.3 Communication skills in negotiation Prepare facts and objectives before the meeting. Ask questions and listen to understand the other party's interests. Use clear, respectful and specific language. Separate people from the problem; avoid personal attacks. Summarise proposals and check that both parties interpret them in the same way. Confirm the final agreement in writing, including responsibilities and dates. Exam tip: Explain negotiation as a process, not merely as bargaining. Include preparation, discussion, agreement and implementation. 11. Communication in a cross-cultural setting Cross-cultural communication takes place when people from different cultural backgrounds interact. Differences in language, values, social expectations, hierarchy, context, time orientation and personal space can influence how a message is expressed and interpreted. 11.1 Cultural sensitivity and cultural intelligence Cultural sensitivity means recognising and respecting cultural differences without treating one's own practices as universal. Cultural intelligence is the ability to understand unfamiliar cultural situations and adapt one's behaviour and communication appropriately. 11.2 High-context and low-context communication High-context tendency More meaning may be carried by context, relationships, shared understanding and non-verbal cues. Messages may be indirect; relationship-building and trust can be important. Low-context tendency More meaning is expressed explicitly through words and detailed explanations. Messages may be direct, explicit and task-focused. These are broad tendencies discussed in intercultural communication literature, not fixed rules about every person in a country. Individuals, organisations and situations differ. 11.3 Areas where cultural differences may affect communication Area Possible misunderstanding Better managerial practice Language Idioms, accents or jargon may be misunderstood. Use plain language; avoid idioms; confirm understanding. Hierarchy and status Some employees may hesitate to disagree openly with a senior person. Invite views respectfully and provide more than one way to give feedback. Time Different expectations about punctuality, deadlines or scheduling. State dates, time zones and deadlines explicitly. Personal space and gestures A gesture or physical distance may be interpreted differently. Observe local expectations and avoid assuming a gesture has universal meaning. Silence and participation Silence may be interpreted as agreement, disagreement or a need for reflection. Ask for explicit confirmation and invite questions. Relationship and trust Some interactions may require relationship-building before business discussion. Allow time to establish rapport and understand expectations. Practical example: virtual meeting across countries A manager asks whether everyone agrees. Nobody objects, so the manager assumes the plan is accepted. Later, team members raise serious concerns. The manager should not interpret silence automatically as agreement. Invite each person to comment, ask specific questions, allow written feedback after the meeting and confirm decisions and action owners in writing. Exam tip: Avoid stereotypes. Explain cultural differences as possible influences, then recommend observing, asking, adapting and confirming understanding. 12. Barriers to effective communication and remedies A communication barrier is any factor that prevents a message from being sent, received or interpreted as intended. Barriers can arise from the sender, message, channel, receiver or organisational environment. Barrier Meaning and example Remedies Incorrect assumptions A manager assumes employees already know a new procedure and gives no explanation. State the context, check prior knowledge, invite questions and confirm understanding. Semantic / language Jargon, ambiguous words or different meanings cause confusion; “ASAP” is interpreted differently. Use simple and precise words, define technical terms and specify dates or quantities. Psychological Fear, anger, stress, prejudice or lack of trust affects how a message is received. Build trust, use respectful language, choose an appropriate time and encourage open discussion. Status / hierarchy Junior employees hesitate to report mistakes or challenge a senior manager. Create safe feedback channels, invite dissent and respond constructively. Cultural Different norms about directness, silence, hierarchy or gestures cause misunderstanding. Develop cultural awareness, avoid stereotypes and clarify expectations. Physical Noise, distance, poor room acoustics or interruptions disrupt communication. Choose a suitable location, reduce distractions and provide written follow-up. Technical / channel Weak internet, microphone failure or a broken link prevents the message from reaching the receiver. Test technology, choose an appropriate channel and keep a backup method. Information overload Too many emails, notices or details make important information difficult to identify. Prioritise key information, use headings and summaries, and send only relevant details. Organisational Too many layers, unclear reporting lines or departmental silos delay information. Clarify communication routes, simplify approvals and encourage cross-functional coordination. Poor listening The receiver interrupts, multitasks or forms conclusions before hearing the full message. Practise active listening, paraphrase, ask questions and remove distractions. Poor message design The message lacks structure, purpose, context or a clear action. Organise the message, state the purpose, identify the action owner and specify the deadline. 12.1 General methods to improve communication Know the audience: consider the receiver's role, knowledge, language and information needs. Choose the right channel: use a meeting for sensitive discussion, a written record for formal decisions and an urgent channel for immediate safety instructions. Use simple and specific language: avoid unnecessary jargon and vague expressions. Encourage feedback: ask the receiver to summarise the action or raise questions. Listen actively: pay attention, avoid interrupting and clarify meaning. Create psychological safety: ensure people can report errors and concerns without unreasonable fear. Reduce information overload: highlight decisions, owners, deadlines and next steps. Follow up: document important decisions and check whether the intended action occurred. High-scoring 10-mark answer: Define the term, classify or list the barriers, explain each with an example, suggest a remedy for each, and conclude that communication must be planned for both sender and receiver. 13. Integrated case studies for application Case 1: Communication breakdown in a hospital A hospital introduces a revised patient-discharge procedure. Administration sends a long circular to all departments. Nurses interpret one step differently from doctors. The pharmacy receives the change late. Patients experience delays and staff blame one another. Questions for students Identify at least four communication problems. Map the failure to the communication process. Which barriers are present? How should downward, upward and horizontal communication be improved? Suggest three immediate managerial actions. Suggested analysis Case 2: Product launch at risk A company discovers a serious defect two days before launch. Engineering wants more time. Marketing has announced the date. Sales has promised customers availability. The product manager must recommend a response. Questions for students Who are the key stakeholders and what information does each need? How should the product manager communicate the issue internally? Which crisis communication principles apply? How can the manager negotiate a revised launch plan? Draft three key points for a customer update without making unsupported promises. Suggested analysis Case 3: Cross-cultural virtual team A manager conducts a virtual meeting with colleagues from several countries. After asking whether everyone agrees, the manager receives no objections and assumes the plan is accepted. Later, team members raise serious concerns. Questions for students Why was silence interpreted incorrectly? Which cross-cultural factors may influence participation? How can the manager invite feedback more effectively?
