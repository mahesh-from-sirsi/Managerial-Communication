1. Oral Communication: Meaning and Nature Oral communication is the exchange of spoken messages between a speaker and a listener. It involves expressing information, ideas, opinions, instructions or feelings through spoken words, supported by voice, tone and non-verbal cues. It is generally immediate and interactive. The listener can ask questions, seek clarification and provide feedback while the conversation is taking place. 1.1 Characteristics of oral communication ●​ Immediate feedback: The speaker can observe the listener's response and clarify the message. ●​ Personal interaction: Voice, facial expression, posture and eye contact add meaning to spoken words. ●​ Flexibility: The speaker can change the explanation, examples or level of detail according to the listener. ●​ Speed: Simple information and urgent instructions can often be communicated quickly. ●​ Possibility of misunderstanding: Accents, noise, poor listening, emotion and ambiguous words may distort meaning. ●​ Limited permanent record: Unless recorded or documented, spoken messages may be forgotten or recalled differently. ●​ Context dependence: Meaning is influenced by the relationship, setting, culture and situation. 1.2 Oral and written communication Basis Oral communication Written communication Usually immediate; questions can be Often delayed; clarification may require Feedback answered at once. another message. 6. Parallel and Sequential Conversation The supplied Chaturvedi material distinguishes parallel conversation from sequential conversation. The distinction is important because a conversation may contain many statements without participants actually responding to one another. Parallel conversation Sequential conversation Each person pushes a separate statement or position. Each response connects logically to what was said before. Participants focus on proving their own point. Participants try to understand and explore the issue. Questions and clarification are limited. Questions and reflection deepen understanding. The discussion is more likely to produce shared understanding The discussion often becomes repetitive or reaches a deadlock. and a decision. 6.1 Example Parallel conversation: Manager: “Customer complaints increased last month.” Sales: “We need more advertising.” Operations: “Our staff is already overloaded.” Sales: “Without advertising we cannot grow.” Operations: “Without more staff we cannot serve customers.” The participants are defending separate positions. They are not examining the underlying problem. Sequential conversation: Manager: “What do the complaints tell us about the main problem?” Sales: “Some customers say the product is difficult to understand.” Operations: “We also see delays in responding to queries.” Manager: “So both product information and response time may be contributing. What evidence can we review first?” Each response builds on the previous one and moves the discussion towards investigation and action. 7. Non-Verbal Communication: Meaning and Nature Non-verbal communication refers to the communication of meaning through cues other than the literal words spoken or written. These cues may include facial expressions, gestures, posture, eye behaviour, voice qualities, appearance, use of space, silence and the use of time. In professional communication, verbal and non-verbal signals usually operate together. Non-verbal behaviour may reinforce, modify or contradict spoken words. 12.1 Characteristics of non-verbal communication ●​ It can accompany spoken words and influence how they are interpreted. ●​ It may communicate attitudes, emotions, attention, confidence or discomfort. ●​ It can be deliberate or unconscious. ●​ Its meaning depends on the person, relationship, culture and situation. ●​ The same cue may have different meanings in different contexts. ●​ It can support or contradict verbal communication, but it is not a guaranteed method of reading another person's mind. 13. 15. Integrated Case Studies Case 1: The Missed Instruction A manager explains a new inventory process during a busy shift. An employee nods throughout the explanation. The next day, the employee follows the old process and says, “I thought you meant only the weekend stock.” Analysis: A nod is not sufficient proof of complete understanding. Background noise and divided attention may have affected listening. The manager could have explained the change in a quieter setting, invited questions and asked the employee to restate the new process. A short written recap could reinforce the oral instruction. Case 2: Parallel Conversation in a Team Meeting A manager reports that customer complaints have increased. Sales argues that more advertising is needed. Operations says that staff are already overloaded. Sales repeats that advertising is essential for growth, while Operations repeats that more staff are needed. The meeting ends without a decision. Analysis: This is parallel conversation because participants defend separate positions rather than exploring the underlying issue. The manager could reflect both concerns, ask what evidence supports each view and guide the group towards a sequential discussion. Case 3: The Interviewer Who Does Not Listen An interviewer asks a candidate a question, checks a laptop while the candidate answers, interrupts after 20 seconds and asks a different question that the candidate has already addressed. Analysis: The interviewer displays poor concentration, interruption and premature evaluation. The non-verbal behaviour may communicate low interest and discourage the candidate from sharing information. The interviewer should listen fully, take brief notes, use appropriate eye behaviour and ask relevant follow-up questions. Case 4: Words and Behaviour Do Not Match During a project review, a manager tells an employee, “Please take your time and explain the issue.” At the same time, the manager repeatedly checks a phone and looks towards the door. The employee gives a very brief explanation and leaves out important details. Analysis: The manager's verbal invitation and non-verbal behaviour send inconsistent signals. The employee may infer that the manager is not available to listen. The manager should remove distractions, give the employee attention and clarify whether there is enough time for the discussion.
